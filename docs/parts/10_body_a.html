SAGE

Deadlock avoidance that learns what each process will do next.

The Simulated Ahead-of-Time Grant Evaluator removes the one thing Banker's algorithm cannot do without: declared maximum demands. Each process gets a behaviour model fitted from its own request log, every request for a free resource is tried in K simulated futures, and only the risky ones wait. This document covers the problem, the decision workflow, the two behaviour engines, a live replay of a real run, the full benchmark against six textbook strategies, the verification suite, the web console and the AI analyst.

I

The problem: deadlock, and the catch in every textbook answer

Two processes, two resources, two lock orders. Each holds one thing and waits for the other, and neither will ever move. An operating system can prevent this, avoid it, or detect it after the fact. Each option costs something.

A deadlock needs four conditions at once: resources that cannot be shared, processes that hold some while waiting for more, no pre-emption, and a circular chain of waiting. Break any one and deadlock is impossible. The textbook strategies differ only in which condition they attack, and when.

1Mutual exclusionA resource serves one process at a time.
2Hold and waitA process keeps what it has while asking for more.
3No pre-emptionNothing is taken away by force.
4Circular waitA cycle in the wait-for graph.
StrategyMechanismWhat it costs
PreventionRule out a condition by design (global lock order, timestamps as in wait-die and wound-wait)Rollbacks of innocent processes, rigid rules
AvoidanceKeep the system in a safe state by refusing requests that could lead to a cycle (Banker's algorithm)Every process must declare its maximum demand in advance
DetectionGrant everything, find cycles in the wait-for graph, abort a victimWasted work and rollbacks every time it happens

Avoidance is the attractive one: no rollbacks, no rigid ordering. Its catch is the declaration. Real programs rarely know, let alone announce, the most they will ever hold. SAGE keeps the avoidance idea and replaces the declaration with something the operating system already has: the history of what each process has asked for.

II

The idea: learn, simulate, then decide

Instead of asking a process what it might need, watch what it does, and before granting a request, play its near future forward many times.

1 · Learn from the request logper process, online, nothing declared

Every request, release and exit a process emits is counted into a behaviour model of that process alone: a Markov chain over its events with two context tables, and optionally a small LSTM that reads its whole history. Predictions are masked to what the process can actually do with what it holds.

2 · Simulate K futures of H stepsa sandboxed rollout from the live state

The allocation state is copied K times with the request granted. In each copy the processes are scheduled uniformly and act by sampling their models for H steps, with exact deadlock detection after every step. The futures run in lock-step as NumPy arrays, so a decision costs a few milliseconds.

3 · Gate on the share that deadlocksrisk ≥ τ means wait

The risk of a grant is the fraction of futures in which the requester ends up on a wait-for cycle. At or above the threshold τ the request is queued and re-evaluated at every release. When the model cannot be trusted, a Banker's-style safety check with learned claims decides instead.

The decision rule, in one line

For a request by process i for a free resource r, with a trusted model:

risk(i, r) = deadlocked futures / K        queue  if  risk ≥ τ
                                             grant  otherwise

With the project defaults, K = futures, H = steps and τ = . The simulation stops early as soon as the comparison with τ is settled: once enough futures have deadlocked to reach τ, or once even every unsettled future deadlocking could not reach it.

Re-evaluationat every release, in arrival order

A queued request is not forgotten. Each release wakes the queue; every waiter whose resource is free is decided again on the current state. Requests the policy has already queued stay queued inside the sandbox too, so one queued request never counts as a threat to another.

Starvation guarddrain, then let the overdue request through

A request that has waited ticks in one go, or a process that has gone ticks without completing a life, is overdue. While one exists, processes that hold nothing are held back. They create no hold-and-wait, and the holders run on and release until the overdue request becomes safe.

Stall handler and recoverywhen nothing is runnable

If every process is waiting and some are queued by policy, the queued request with the lowest re-scored risk is force-granted. A deadlock that still occurs is detected exactly and resolved by aborting the cycle member aborted least often, rolled back only to the last point at which it held nothing.

Simulating…

The rollout engine keeps all K futures in one set of arrays of shape (K, n, m) and advances them together: blocked processes acquire their resource as soon as it is free, one runnable process per future is drawn uniformly and samples its next event from its model, restricted to what it can do with what it holds, and after every step the futures in which the requester waits are checked for a cycle. A future is settled when it deadlocks or when the requester finishes its life.

Because the policy only compares the risk with τ, the simulation stops as soon as that comparison can no longer change. That is why queued decisions often show fewer than K futures examined: the verdict was already certain.
